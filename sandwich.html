UAV Sandwich Panel Geometry Optimization

Project Overview

This project investigates how the internal geometry of lightweight sandwich panels influences structural stiffness, load-bearing capacity, failure behavior, and mass efficiency for unmanned aerial vehicle applications. Conducted in collaboration with Genevieve Hill as part of Harvard's ES192 Materials Selection and Design course, the investigation combines parametric CAD modeling, additive manufacturing, finite element analysis, and experimental mechanical testing.

The study compares four additively manufactured lattice-core geometries against a conventional aramid-paper honeycomb reference. By evaluating different internal load-bearing architectures under consistent dimensional and loading conditions, the project aims to identify configurations that maximize bending stiffness and load capacity relative to structural mass.

Engineering Objective

Aerospace sandwich panels use stiff outer face sheets separated by a lightweight core to resist bending without the mass penalty of a solid structure. The face sheets primarily carry bending-induced tensile and compressive stresses, while the core maintains separation between the skins and transfers transverse shear loads. Core geometry therefore plays an important role in overall panel stiffness, stability, and structural efficiency.

The primary objective is to determine which internal lattice configuration provides the greatest bending stiffness and load-bearing capacity relative to specimen mass. The investigation also considers whether geometries that improve absolute stiffness introduce additional mass or manufacturing complexity that reduces their practical benefit.

Performance Metrics

  • Bending stiffness
  • Maximum load capacity
  • Specimen mass
  • Specific stiffness
  • Structural failure mode
  • Manufacturability and dimensional accuracy

Specific Stiffness

Because the candidate geometries contain different quantities of material, stiffness alone is not sufficient for comparing structural efficiency. The measured load-displacement stiffness will therefore be normalized by specimen mass.

k = F / δ
Specific Stiffness = k / m

Core Geometry Design

Four alternative lattice architectures were selected for comparison against a conventional aramid-paper honeycomb core. Each geometry uses a different internal arrangement of structural members or surfaces, allowing the investigation to evaluate the influence of topology on load distribution, deformation, and structural efficiency.

Geometry Structural Characteristics
Aramid Honeycomb Conventional sandwich-core reference configuration.
Triangular Lattice Triangulated network of interconnected load-bearing members.
Reinforced Kelvin Lattice Cellular lattice incorporating additional structural reinforcement.
Gyroidal Structure Continuous interconnected surface-based architecture.
Reinforced Auxetic Truss Reinforced re-entrant lattice geometry with potential auxetic deformation behavior.

Individual unit cells have been developed in Onshape using standardized geometric parameters. These unit cells are patterned into larger lattice assemblies for manufacturing and structural evaluation.

Specimen Design & Materials

The current specimen design uses standardized lattice dimensions and unit-cell parameters to support controlled comparisons between different geometries. Each lattice is intended to be assembled with carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer face sheets to create a complete sandwich structure.

CAD Development & Prototyping

Initial development has focused on modeling individual lattice unit cells in Onshape and establishing repeatable geometries for full-scale specimen fabrication. Unit cells were designed according to the specified dimensional constraints and assembled into 3 × 3 × 9 lattice patterns.

Preliminary manufacturing trials have been conducted using FDM 3D printing. Unit-cell prototypes have been fabricated with and without support structures, and additional specimens have been printed at 1.5 times the nominal scale to investigate manufacturing differences and associated mass changes. Lattice assemblies have also been printed at the intended 1:1 scale.

These trials are being used to identify manufacturing constraints associated with thin lattice members, support removal, print orientation, and dimensional accuracy. Subsequent fabrication will involve refining FDM parameters and manufacturing nylon specimens using SLS.

Structural Simulation & Failure Analysis

Preliminary finite element analysis has begun in ANSYS using both individual unit-cell geometries and larger lattice assemblies. Initial simulations investigate bending response, structural deformation, and stress distributions to identify regions where additional reinforcement may improve performance.

Particular attention has been given to the reinforced Kelvin lattice, where simulation results are being used to inform the placement of additional structural members. This process allows potential design modifications to be evaluated before committing to additional manufacturing trials.

Buckling & Failure Modes

The next stage of ANSYS analysis will incorporate buckling loads and more representative boundary conditions to investigate anticipated failure mechanisms. Potential failure modes include localized truss buckling, excessive bending deformation, core shear failure, and instability within reinforced lattice members.

Simulation Outputs

  • Stress distributions
  • Maximum structural displacement
  • Effective bending stiffness
  • Potential buckling locations
  • Structural mass
  • Predicted failure regions

These simulations will provide a basis for comparing the structural behavior of each geometry and will later be evaluated against experimental measurements.

Mechanical Testing & Validation

Following fabrication of the remaining lattice configurations and CFRP face sheets, complete sandwich panel specimens will be assembled for comparative mechanical testing. The experimental methodology includes three-point bending using an Instron testing machine to measure applied force, midspan displacement, maximum load capacity, and structural failure behavior.

Specimens will be tested under consistent support and loading conditions, with mass recorded independently to calculate specific stiffness. Repeated specimens will be used to assess variability within each configuration. Results will be compared with ANSYS predictions to evaluate the accuracy of the structural models and identify discrepancies between simulated and observed failure behavior.

Experimental Measurements

Measurement Purpose
Force–Displacement Response Determine effective bending stiffness.
Maximum Applied Load Compare load-bearing capacity before failure.
Specimen Mass Calculate specific stiffness.
Failure Mode Identify structural weaknesses and compare against FEA predictions.
Dimensional Accuracy Evaluate manufacturing consistency.

Current Progress & Next Steps

Current progress includes parametric development of lattice unit cells in Onshape, preliminary FDM manufacturing trials at nominal and enlarged scales, and initial ANSYS structural simulations. The reinforced Kelvin lattice is being evaluated to identify potential reinforcement locations and improve its bending response.

Upcoming work includes completing the remaining lattice geometries, refining print settings for improved dimensional accuracy, incorporating buckling analysis into ANSYS, manufacturing nylon specimens using SLS, fabricating CFRP face sheets, and assembling complete sandwich panels. Mechanical testing will then be conducted to compare stiffness, mass efficiency, maximum load, and failure behavior across the selected configurations.
