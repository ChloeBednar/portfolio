Aerostructures Independent Research

Project Overview

AEROFRAME is an independent research investigation driven by my interest in aerostructural design, computational aerodynamics, and unconventional aircraft configurations.

The project investigates whether intentional structural cavities within an aircraft can improve overall aerodynamic and propulsive efficiency while maintaining sufficient structural integrity. Specifically, I'm exploring a bifurcated, flow-through aircraft configuration incorporating a centrally aligned rear-mounted turbofan. The proposed geometry allows incoming airflow to pass through a central cavity before reaching the engine inlet.

The central objective is to determine whether modifying the aircraft's upstream geometry can improve engine inlet flow conditions and aircraft-level efficiency relative to a conventional enclosed configuration and the investigation combines aerodynamic modeling, structural analysis, parametric geometry optimization, and planned experimental validation.

Preliminary comparison of conventional enclosed and bifurcated flow-through aircraft configurations

Research Hypothesis

A bifurcated, flow-through aircraft configuration with a centrally aligned rear-mounted turbofan inlet may improve aircraft-level aeropropulsive efficiency by modifying the upstream flow field and reducing aerodynamic losses associated with conventional enclosed airframe geometries.

Through optimization of cavity cross-sectional area, longitudinal curvature, and inlet transition geometry, the configuration may achieve favorable inlet total-pressure recovery and flow uniformity while maintaining acceptable aerodynamic drag, structural mass, bending stiffness, and torsional rigidity. This investigation seeks to determine whether the aerodynamic and propulsive benefits of an integrated flow-through configuration outweigh additional wetted-area drag, flow interference, and structural penalties introduced by the cavity.

Primary Research Question

Can an aerodynamically optimized bifurcated airframe improve rear-mounted turbofan inlet performance and overall aircraft efficiency relative to a conventional enclosed configuration?

Secondary Research Question

Can these aerodynamic and propulsive benefits be achieved without unacceptable reductions in structural stiffness, strength, or mass efficiency?

Aerodynamic Design Considerations

Introducing a flow-through cavity changes several interacting aerodynamic characteristics. A reduction in frontal blockage does not necessarily produce a reduction in total drag, particularly when additional internal surfaces and flow interactions are introduced.

Frontal Blockage

Removing portions of the enclosed airframe may reduce projected frontal blockage. However, the resulting pressure distribution and drag depend on the complete external geometry rather than frontal area alone.

Wetted Surface Area

Flow-through cavities introduce additional surfaces exposed to the freestream. Boundary-layer development along these surfaces produces skin-friction drag, which must be considered when evaluating the potential benefits of the geometry.

Interference Drag

The intersection of cavity walls, structural members, and the engine inlet may introduce local pressure gradients, boundary-layer thickening, flow separation, and vortical structures that increase aerodynamic losses.

Engine Inlet Flow Quality

Because the proposed cavity terminates near a rear-mounted turbofan inlet, its geometry may influence the velocity distribution, total-pressure recovery, and flow angularity of air entering the propulsion system. The objective is not simply to accelerate airflow toward the engine, but to deliver favorable inlet conditions while minimizing losses and maintaining acceptable flow uniformity.

Airfoil Screening & Optimization

The initial aerodynamic investigation focuses on screening candidate airfoil geometries using XFOIL. Candidate profiles are evaluated across prescribed Reynolds numbers, lift coefficients, and angles of attack to establish preliminary sectional aerodynamic characteristics. Initial candidate airfoils include NACA 0012, NACA 2412, NACA 64-210, and NASA SC(2)-0410.

Airfoil Evaluation Parameters

  • Lift coefficient and lift-curve slope
  • Profile drag at a target lift coefficient of 0.40
  • Lift-to-drag ratio and aerodynamic efficiency
  • Pitching moment and associated trim requirements
  • Thickness-to-chord ratio and structural integration constraints
  • Surface pressure distributions, suction peaks, and pressure recovery
  • Maximum lift coefficient and stall characteristics

XFOIL results will be used to narrow the candidate airfoil geometries before progressing to three-dimensional aerodynamic modeling. Because the intended cruise condition is Mach 0.85, XFOIL is being used for preliminary screening rather than definitive transonic analysis. Shock formation, compressibility effects, and wave drag will require subsequent higher-fidelity CFD simulations.

3D Aerodynamic Modeling

Following preliminary airfoil screening, the investigation will progress to three-dimensional aerodynamic modeling using XFLR5. This stage will examine wing planform geometry, spanwise lift distribution, aspect ratio, and induced drag under conditions appropriate for preliminary low-speed aerodynamic analysis. A full parametric aircraft model will then be developed to compare conventional enclosed and bifurcated flow-through configurations. Both configurations will incorporate a centrally located rear-mounted turbofan inlet to establish a consistent basis for evaluating airframe-propulsion interactions.

Geometric Design Variables

  • Cavity width and height
  • Cavity cross-sectional area
  • Longitudinal cavity curvature
  • Cavity convergence and divergence
  • Engine inlet transition geometry
  • Structural branching configuration
  • Distribution of load-bearing members

The goal is to establish geometrically consistent configurations for comparative aerodynamic and structural evaluation.

Engine Inlet Integration & Flow Optimization

A central component of AEROFRAME is investigating how the upstream airframe geometry influences airflow delivered to a rear-mounted turbofan engine. The proposed configuration positions the engine inlet near the downstream opening of the central cavity, allowing the cavity geometry to influence the incoming flow field. Preliminary inlet sizing will be established using a representative turbofan operating point, including required engine mass flow and inlet capture area.

Engine Mass-Flow Requirements

A simplified inlet capture-area estimate can be obtained from the continuity equation:

ṁ = ρAV

The relationship between required engine mass flow, local air density, and flow velocity will inform preliminary cavity and inlet sizing.

Total-Pressure Recovery

Inlet total-pressure recovery will be evaluated to determine how effectively the cavity preserves incoming flow total pressure.

πd = Pt,inlet / Pt,∞

Higher total-pressure recovery generally indicates lower inlet losses, although pressure recovery alone does not determine overall propulsive efficiency.

Inlet Flow Distortion

In addition to average pressure recovery, the investigation will evaluate nonuniformities in total pressure and velocity distributions entering the engine. Circumferential and radial pressure distortion, flow angularity, and localized separation will be considered when assessing inlet flow quality.

Computational Fluid & Structural Analysis

Full-aircraft configurations will be evaluated using ANSYS computational fluid dynamics and finite element analysis to quantify aerodynamic, propulsion-integration, and structural performance.

CFD

Compressible-flow simulations will investigate airflow through the central cavity and into the rear-mounted turbofan inlet. The analysis will examine pressure distributions, velocity fields, shock formation, boundary-layer development, flow separation, and interference effects. Engine inlet boundary conditions will be established using a representative engine operating point to maintain consistent mass-flow requirements across the candidate geometries. Simulations will compare total-pressure recovery, inlet distortion, aerodynamic drag, and sensitivity to changes in cavity geometry and angle of attack.

Finite Element Analysis

Structural simulations will evaluate stress distributions, deformation, bending stiffness, torsional rigidity, and potential failure regions under representative aerodynamic loading conditions. Particular attention will be given to structural load paths surrounding the central cavity, where the removal of enclosed material may introduce stress concentrations or reduce stiffness. Structural mass and stiffness will be compared against the enclosed baseline to determine whether aerodynamic improvements justify the associated structural trade-offs.

Comparative simulations will evaluate the relationship between inlet performance, aircraft drag, structural mass, and stiffness. The ultimate objective is to identify configurations that improve overall aircraft-level performance rather than optimizing isolated aerodynamic or structural characteristics.

Experimental Validation

Following computational evaluation, selected aircraft configurations will be fabricated as scaled physical models using additive manufacturing. I plan to design and construct a desktop wind tunnel to experimentally investigate aerodynamic performance and airflow behavior across the candidate configurations. The experimental setup will incorporate a controlled suction system or simplified inlet flow simulation to investigate airflow entering the rear-mounted engine inlet. Testing will focus on comparative aerodynamic forces, inlet pressure distributions, flow behavior, and sensitivity to changes in cavity geometry. Experimental results will be compared against corresponding low-speed computational predictions to evaluate model accuracy and guide subsequent design iterations. Reynolds-number similarity, tunnel blockage, model scaling, and measurement uncertainty will be considered during testing.

Current Progress

AEROFRAME is currently in the preliminary aerodynamic design and airfoil screening stage. My initial work has focused on defining the research hypothesis, establishing reference flight conditions, performing preliminary aircraft sizing calculations, and developing aerodynamic evaluation criteria. Current analysis involves screening candidate airfoil geometries in XFOIL, with subsequent work planned in XFLR5, parametric CAD modeling, ANSYS CFD/FEA, and scaled experimental validation. As the investigation progresses, computational results, aerodynamic comparisons, structural simulations, and experimental findings will be incorporated into this project documentation.
